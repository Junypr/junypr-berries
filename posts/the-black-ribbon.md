I was in New York, for an interview, for the first time in I think nearly a decade. They'd flown me out from LA a day early, so I'd spent my morning at the MoMA, staring at a pile of chocolate candies, thinking about the artist's friend who'd been taken by AIDS. I couldn't bring myself to take one.

Then I'd called him again, and asked if he wanted to meet me on the Highline just before sunset. He said yes.

I found a bench and sat down with the bag of pastries I'd grabbed from Chinatown.

His name was Jupiter — or Ju, to me. It was his chosen name. He was a senior studying game design at NYU.

I knew he'd be late.

The city was bathed in that funny late-summer glow that bronzed people's cheeks. It turned the cornflowers by the bench white-gold. I put the bag of pastries on my lap and closed my eyes. It was nice to be in an actual city.

"Fancy seeing you here."

He was wearing a black-and-gold embroidered waistcoat over a white button up and charcoal slacks. There was a black ribbon tied around his throat. He had new steel bracelets, and a ring in the shape of a viper's head.

He scanned me up and down. We used to look pretty similar, before he transitioned. But I'd grown my hair out, and he'd chopped his. I was wearing a black blouse and jeans, and he — well, I'd never seen him in anything other than khakis and graphic tees before.

"Hey," I said. "Sit. I haven't seen you in what — four years?"

He tapped his chin. "Has it really been that long?"

"I mean, you weren't at graduation right? And I don't remember seeing you most of senior year."

"Jeez... I didn't realize it'd been so long since..." He took the fried sesame ball I was offering. "I don't think anyone from high school remembers me."

"Well I don't think anyone from there remembers me either."

"People remember you. You know, I went back to our middle school and told our old teachers about you, and they said they were proud of you."

"I think they're just being nice." I took a fat bite out of my sesame ball. "To be fair, I'm surprised they recognized *you*. Like, you chopped your hair and changed your style and everything."

"I'm still the same autistic dumbass though." He took a bite of his sesame ball. "Never had this," he said through a mouthful of red bean paste. 

"It's good isn't it?" I swallowed. "I like your outfit, by the way. Where'd you get the rings?"

"Thanks. Etsy — there's this artist I like."

"Send me the link sometime." I took another, tiny bite. "So what's up with that ribbon around your neck? Doesn't really go with the rest of the fit."

"Oh, I just felt like it."

"Really?"

"Yeah, it goes with my loafers."

"I thought you wouldn't even wear lanyards because they bothered you."

He shrugged. "I got used to it. This sesame ball's good. You know, I keep thinking about going to Chinatown, but I never got around to it, and it's been three years."

"Stuck in your dorm room all day?"

"Sometimes. I've been working on a game with some friends, have I shown you?"

He pulled out his tablet and started flipping through concept art. 

"Damn," I whispered. "Your artist's good."

He laughed before returning to scrolling. "Well that stuff was me. I spent so much time on that fucking tree. Bark is such a pain to render."

"Dang. I forgot how good you are." I leaned back. The sun had vanished behind the skyline. "You got more white hairs?"

"Well I guess I did. Didn't notice until you pointed it out."

"You doing ok?"

"Yeah yeah."

"I guess you don't really go home much do you?"

"Did I tell you I got disowned? I really try not to go there unless I have to. I was here for the summer. New York's fucking expensive, but I shared a room with a friend. She's great, she's an aerospace major like you. You'd like her."

"Sorry, you got disowned?"

"Oh I didn't tell you? I'm paying my own tuition now. Well, taking out a loan for it. But it's fine. Don't worry about me." He grins.

"Shit I'm sorry —"

"No no it's fine. I don't want any obligations to them anyway."

"I guess that's fair. It still sucks though."

"I never really liked LA much anyway. You'd think I would, with my line of work. But it just... I don't know. There's something about it. I love New York City though. Except the weather. But the food's amazing."

"Are you really experiencing the food if you haven't been to Chinatown though?" I raised an eyebrow. He laughed. "But seriously, though, I'm happy for you. LA was too big and too small for you."

"What do you mean?"

"Well I don't know. Like all those people we graduated with — like, they were just happy, being there. Like, they didn't fucking care. But you're — you're a big person and you want big things and like. LA's just kinda fucking empty. It's big, but it's also so fucking small. So fucking the same. Like, it felt like there wasn't even space for you."

"Well my fat ass could not fit in all of LA."

"Oh my god don't call yourself fat. Don't do this again."

"I was compared to the girls from high school."

"Ju..."

"You were pretty. People liked you."

"I wasn't even. And all those guys were assholes."

"Whatever you say." He finished the rest of his sesame ball in one bite and picked the seeds off his fingers. "So what brings you to New York?"

"You." I laugh. "No, interview."

"There're aerospace companies in New York?"

"I'm not interviewing for an aerospace company."

"What is it then?"

"Finance." I looked away. "You probably don't know the company."

"I thought you were an aerospace major. I thought you wanted to build planes."

"Yeah, and then I wanted to get paid. Who knew good money only comes from selling your soul." I reached into the bag and grab two flaky winter melon pastries, passing one to him. "And they're nice, really. Smart people. They paid for the flights and the hotel and the car from the airport. They sent an Escalade, Ju. I've never been in an Escalade before. I don't know. Younger me would not be proud. But like, I've realized I actually really want it, and it kinda scares me."

"Capitalism likes soulless robots. You can't think about it too much if you want money."

"Yeah but I kinda judge myself." I bit into the pastry, relishing the sugary crunch of the candied melon. "I wish I had your courage to commit to what I actually wanted to do."

"If I had your brains I'd go after the money too. But I'm too dumb for that."

"You could've, Ju. You're not dumb. You certainly weren't when we first met." I thought about him, in middle school. He was the star of the class. 

"Yeah, and then I —"

"And you can recover from that, you know."

"My mom used to compare me to you. 'She can do it, so why can't you?' All the time. And I'd see you do everything she wanted. You'd charm people. Get A's without breaking a sweat. I was angry. And I took it out on you."

"I... I mean..."

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry I lashed out at you. I'm sorry I started that fight in the friend group. I'm sorry I excluded you. It wasn't even your fault. You were always nice to me. I never apologized. I just couldn't do it to your face."

"Hey look. It's fine, ok? I'm just glad you're ok now." I wrapped the pastry in some napkins and put my hand on his knee. "Hey, can I tell you something? On the flight here, we had all these maintenance issues, and I was just sitting there thinking, if the plane goes down, and I don't survive, honestly, I don't care. Like, sometimes I just don't care if I live or die. Because there will be nothing to worry about. And I kinda don't mind that. And then I got here and was like fuck. What was I thinking? There's so much here. So much life. So much to see and do and taste. And I don't mean to say I have experienced even a fraction of what you've gone through but. I understand. I understand how much it fucks with you. It's ok."

He touched the ribbon at his neck. "I'd have to cross the train tracks after school. It would always be right before the 3:05 train. And the bells would start ringing just as I'd approach the tracks. And I'd — I'd make a game of it. Duck under the gate arm, run across, knowing that if the train hit me, I'd —"

"Ju —" I squeezed his knee. 

"I did this for years. Like I was playing with the train. Daring it to be a little too fast —"

"But nothing happened, right?"

"Do you remember the last day you saw me?" His fingers twisted around the loose ends of the ribbon's bow. 

I looked down. "You just kept showing up less and less..."

"But the last day?"

"Sometime in November..."

"I was trying to run across the tracks. My backpack got caught on the gate arm. I fell." He was pulling on the ends of the ribbon. I watched the loops shrink, smaller and smaller.

"But you're here. You're fine."

"The train didn't stop. It couldn't have."

I got a flash of him sprawled on the tracks in front of me. The train's horn. The chugging of the wheels. 

The ribbon dropped. His neck snapped. His head rolled off his shoulders and into the cornflowers. His hands still clutched the half-eaten pastry.

I screamed.

I screamed, and ran, and when I was done running, I called the company and told them that I was very sorry, that I'd refund the plane tickets and the hotel and the fancy car there, that I just couldn't do the interview with them. They didn't really understand, but they let me.

And though I've gone back to New York since, I've never had the courage to visit that part of the Highline. I checked the news that night — there was no mention of a severed head found among the cornflowers — but I still hold an irrational fear that I'll see his body, sitting on that bench, hands clutching a now-moldy pastry.

Or maybe he's reaffixed his head and finished his degree at NYU, and is now working the job of his dreams. 

I know I didn't dream it. Because when I returned to my hotel that night, I found a black ribbon in my pocket.

I know what I should really do. But I'm too scared to try to call. Too scared of who — or what — might answer.

So I'll stay, at this job near LA, building airplanes, not thinking too much about people from my past.
