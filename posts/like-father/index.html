Like Father

Abby balanced the newest letter on top of the neat stack of business envelopes banished to the bottom drawer of her desk. Each had the same return address and name: Mark Li, her father. She’d been promising herself that she’d read them someday. Just not today. Every time she saw his type-writer handwriting, she remembered their final conversation. “I never loved you,” she’d said. It’d been three years, but the heat of those words still rose to her cheeks in a flush.

She cradled a steaming cup of tea and frowned at the tower. The handwriting on the newest letter was shaky, like the scrawl of a child just learning to write, and the corners of the envelope were crumpled. Mark hadn’t been the most attentive to detail, but he knew the value of a first impression. Abby pinched the bridge of her nose, flicked open a pocket knife, and slid the blade under the envelope flap. Maybe today was the day. 

What would the words inside say? Some twisted apology? Justification, more likely, in precise, mathematical statements. She dropped the envelope and shut the pocketknife. She couldn’t deal with it. Not now. She had work to do.

Abby opened a notebook to a page covered in her careless scrawl. She’d clean the proof up once she had everything figured out. She just needed to step back and see how it all connected. 

Abby’s phone rang. She checked the caller id, and nearly slammed her phone face down onto the desk. Vivienne Li. Funny how at twenty, a junior in college, she still felt her mother’s hawkish eyes on her back. 

Abby put the phone on speaker. “Hello?”

“We need to talk.”

Abby crossed a line out in her notebook and ripped out a fresh page. “Ok.”

“Abby, don't talk to me like that. Can’t you be happy for once?”

“All I said was ok.”

“You have a terrible attitude. You’re always so angry at me.”

Abby ripped the frayed edge of the paper. “Me? You’re the one who takes out their anger on me.”

“Abby, just listen.”

“Don’t you realize this is why I wanted to get away? Why I decided to come here? You think I enjoy the six hour flight back?”

“Listen to me.”

“Why don’t you just divorce that goddamn man if you hate him so much?”

“Can you just listen to me?” Vivienne smashed her fist into a table.

The last time Vivienne had lashed out was years ago. “Yes, mother,” Abby said.

“Abby, your father is dying. Have you been reading the letters?”

“I —” Abby stared at the stack of envelopes. Not even one. She could’ve opened one. “What happened to him?”

“Brain cancer. He was diagnosed last spring.”

“But — why didn’t you tell me?”

“You were busy. I didn’t want to bother you.”

“But—”

“We didn’t think it would happen so fast.”

“I — I’m sorry.”

“You need to come home.”

Abby stared at the wall calendar, a gift from her father that she kept only because she hadn’t had the time to find one she actually liked. She wasn’t five anymore; she didn’t appreciate the unicorn staring back at her. She wanted to tear its glittery mane to shreds. “Ok,” Abby said before she hung up.

Abby watched the swirls of steam drift away from her cup of tea. Her friend had given her the tea bags, not realizing that they were the exact kind her father had drunk when he still lived with them. She hated how much she liked it. She hated how every sip brought her back to when she was six, when he’d mentioned that he was working on a theorem in a new field called extremal combinatorics. She’d begged him to explain it to her, and was delighted when he agreed. That was the reason she’d decided to pursue mathematics. Back when he seemed to believe in her. Before he moved back to China to teach mathematics at a university with his fancy American degree, leaving his daughter and wife behind. 

Abby booked herself the morning flight. She’d have to wake up at 4 am and ask for a makeup for tomorrow’s midterm. Her meticulously planned week would be thrown completely off course. But she could deal with that. Abby grabbed the stack of letters and shoved them into Friday night’s plastic take-out bag. She needed to go home.

#

The plane was cold. Abby trembled in her favorite oversized sweater, pressing her eyes closed and resting her forehead against the plastic seat back in front of her, listening to the hum of the engines. Sleep was futile. So was working on her proof. Abby pulled the first envelope out of the plastic bag and tore it open with her fingernails. 

To Abby,

The birds are beginning to come down. They’re beautiful. I know you’d like to listen to them. Missing you so much. I’m so proud of you.

Dad

The letter was dated from a few weeks ago; the handwriting was clumsy. Abby scolded herself as her face grew hot. The last time he’d said he was proud of her was the day she had gotten into her dream college. Her mother had taken her to dinner at her favorite ramen spot. He’d put on a grin and congratulated her. But that night, he pulled her aside and asked how many Ivies she’d heard back from. 

#

Abby had offered to take an Uber home from the airport, but her mother insisted on driving her. It felt almost normal, sitting in the passenger seat as if she were being picked up from school. Vivienne gripped the steering, eyes flicking back and forth. She tensed as a SUV cut in front of them without using its turn signal. 

Abby rested her hand on her mother’s forearm. “Let me drive.”

“No. It’s fine.”

“You’re going to get into an accident.”

“I said it’s fine.”

The backs of Vivienne’s hands were cracked and raw from years of cooking and cleaning. Abby had never known her to be anything but a mother, but the few friends Vivienne had came from her days as a researcher in mathematics. Abby imagined Vivienne’s hands dry with chalk dust, the life she could’ve had if Mark hadn’t moved back to China, forcing her to raise Abby alone. Maybe she’d have been happier if she hadn’t had to give it all up. 

“Be nice to your father,” Vivienne said.

“What?”

“Don’t look like that. You look grumpy. Be nice.”

Abby balled her hands into fists. “Ok.”

“Not like the last time you saw him.”

“Ok.” That was last Christmas. They’d exchanged a dozen words over the two weeks she’d been home. 

Vivienne parked the car outside and gave Abby a warning glance before unlocking the door. Abby’s father sat at the kitchen table with a cup and a magazine, turning at the sound of Abby’s footsteps. “Where’s your mother?” Vivienne appeared by his side, hands folded. “This tea is too cold.”

“You said it was too hot when I left.”

“It’s too cold now.”

He lifted the cup, hands shaking. Golden liquid sloshed onto the table. Vivienne sighed and took the cup from his hands. “Abby, can you get a paper towel?”

Abby grabbed a roll off the shelf and started mopping, careful not to brush against her father. It’d been years since they’d had a proper conversation. 

“How’s Harvard?”

Abby dropped the soggy paper towel on the table. “I don’t go to Harvard.”

Vivienne returned with a fresh mug of steaming tea that smelled exactly like the cup Abby had left on her desk.

“Not those paper towels,” Vivienne said.

Abby turned. “What?”

“Use the ones in the kitchen. Never mind. Next time I’ll do it. Come with me.”

Abby followed Vivienne into the kitchen. When Abby had lived there, there was always a pot burbling on the stove: a clay vessel of her favorite stewed pork ribs and eggs, or rice congee, or at least a stir fry. Now, there were no dishes in the drying rack, not even a stray pair of chopsticks — just a pile of paper plates and plastic forks in the trash can and a half opened package of microwavable lasagna on the counter. 

Vivienne dropped the soggy paper towel into the trash can. “He’s been like this for the past few months. If you don’t want him to get grumpy at you then act happy.”

“Ok.”

“You aren’t very good at acting happy.”

“What do you want me to do?”

“Never mind. I’ll find a way to keep him away from you.”

“I can help. I know how to cook now.”

“It’s fine.”

“Let me help.”

“You’ll do it wrong.”

“Please.”

Vivienne reached for the cling wrap and began methodically covering the lasagna. 

“You asked me to be here for a reason. Let me help you,” Abby said.

Vivienne paused. “Don’t burn the rice.”

#

Vivienne shook her head at Abby’s fried rice. “Is this what you’ve been eating in college? It’s not very healthy. Too much oil.” She poked at a singed rice clump with her chopsticks. “And it’s burnt.”

“I like it,” Mark said.

Strange. Mark had never been one for “poor people’s” dishes, preferring salmon and steak when he came home. Perhaps to spite him, Vivienne always overcooked the meat. 

“What have you been doing in college?” Mark asked.

“Classes.” Abby offered her favorite deadpan response.

“That sounds boring.”

“Abby’s classes are very important,” Vivienne said.

Abby lifted her chin, meeting his gaze for the first time in years. “I’m also researching extremal combinatorics.”

“I’ve never heard of that.”

“What?” Surely Mark hadn’t forgotten the theorem that made his name.

“Will you explain it to me?”

Abby looked at Vivienne, who returned a blank, confused stare. “I — I can.”

#

After washing the dishes, Abby flopped onto her childhood bed. The blankets were still rumpled from the end of the winter break and smelled like they’d been abandoned for too long. She grabbed another envelope from the bag and ripped it open. This letter was dated from the beginning of her freshman year. 

To my daughter,

I don’t understand the way you act. I don’t like the way you treated me before you left. You know I love you. I kept you fed and clothed. I’m paying your tuition. Why can’t you show me the same? 

Your father

Abby closed her eyes, remembering that night in her senior fall. She’d heard them arguing in Chinese in the kitchen. They thought she couldn’t understand. 

“She’s good at math, but she’ll never be enough. She’s a girl,” Mark said.

Vivienne’s voice was barely above a whisper. “You have no idea what she’s done in the past few years.”

“Who will continue the family name?”

“You want a son? I already told you. It’s not happening. She’s enough.”

Abby refused to look at either of them when she came down for dinner. He’d been on the other end of the oversized table, staring at Abby in a way that made her feel objectified. 

“Abby, how’s your day been?” he asked.

Abby kept her gaze on the wine stewed pork ribs in her bowl. “Like you care.”

Mark set his chopsticks down. "I care about you, Abby. I'm your father. Don't treat me like this. I thought I taught you respect."

"You didn't teach me anything."

“Abby,” Vivienne warned.

“Why can’t we just be a happy family?” Mark said. “I just want to sit down at this table and have a good conversation.”

Abby crushed an egg between her chopsticks. “A happy family? You come home twice a year, knowing nothing about us, and you expect us to be a happy family? You don’t even care about me. You just want a son.” Abby returned Vivienne’s stare. “You think I can’t understand you?”

Mark slammed his fist into the table. “Don't speak to me like that.” He turned to Vivienne. “She is disrespecting me. Do something.”

Vivienne slouched around her bowl, forehead resting on her fingertips. “I don’t think —”

“You don’t think what?”

Vivienne closed her eyes.

“That’s right,” Mark said. “Side with her like you always do. Sometimes I think you love her more than me.”

“You left.” Vivienne said.

“I left because I had to. I had to support this family. I made sacrifices for this family. I just want this family to appreciate me.”

Abby stood, kicking her chair backwards. “You know what? You think I don’t love you? You asshole. I never did.”

“Abby, finish your dinner.” Vivienne said.

Abby ran up the stairs.

“Abby, get back here.”

Abby locked the door and fell backwards onto her bed. When she was sure Vivienne wasn’t following her, she pressed her face into the pillow and let the tears leak from her eyes. She’d cried herself to sleep that night. 

Abby folded the letter and placed it back into the envelope. She stared at the neat print of her name on the envelope's back. Then she pulled the letter out of the envelope and tore it to shreds.

#

Abby brought a pencil and paper down to the table with her the next morning. At the bottom of the stairs, she paused, trembling. 

Mark sat framed in a square of light spilling from the garden window, spooning rice congee into his mouth. He spilled a bit of each spoonful back into the bowl. Vivienne was beside him, wiping his chin. When Abby cleared her throat, she looked up, met Abby’s eyes, and nodded.

Abby placed the paper on the table and picked up the pencil, beginning to explain the theorem that she’d been working on.

Mark stopped her after a few lines, circling a term with his fingertip. “Can you explain this?”

Abby pressed her lips together. He’d had little patience for her interruptions when she was little, taking each hesitation as proof she wasn’t “enough” to be the mathematician he was. All she’d ever wanted was to be enough.

Abby began to elaborate. She wouldn’t be like him.

#

Abby stood in the kitchen, washing bok choy for the night's noodle soup. The bags under her eyes were beginning to smooth out. She'd explained most of the proof's lemmas to Mark, but hadn't figured out how to fit them together. Something for tomorrow.

Vivienne appeared behind her, inspecting her handiwork. Abby took her silence to indicate approval.

Just before Vivienne turned away, Abby whispered, "He seems so calm around me. Does he remember?"

"Let's not talk about it."

#

It was the third day Abby had been home, and she was at the breakfast table, hunched over the paper now filled with her dense, loopy writing. She didn't know how to prove the conclusion to him when she couldn't quite grasp it herself.

Mark cleared his throat. "Maybe I could help."

"I don't —"

"I think I understand it now."

Abby surrendered the paper and pencil. Mark held the pencil in his fist and began scratching arrows. Abby cringed as her orderly, dense print was marred.

"There's three cases right?"

Abby nodded.

"The first two have been essentially proved here and here." Mark circled a series of lemmas.

"But the last one?" Abby asked.

"It could never happen. That's basically what this last lemma says."

"Oh my god." Abby snatched the sheet of paper and ran upstairs to copy down Mark's revelation before it fell apart in her head.

#

That night, after dinner, Abby sat in bed, her head between her knees, breathing hard. Beside her laid the plastic takeout bag of unopened letters. In her hand was a sheet of paper dated from about a year ago.

Dear Abby,

I know I was never the best father. I know I was never there when you needed me. I'm sorry. I don't have much time left, and it's made me realize that I barely got to know you.

I found a few photographs of us from when you were younger. I hope you'll look at them and smile.

Mark

Polaroids were scattered across the duvet. A man with wire framed glasses and salt and pepper hair carrying a toddler piggyback. The same girl at a whiteboard, proudly pointing at 1 + 1 = 11. Him behind her as she blew out red birthday candles.

#

Abby didn't have to come down to breakfast wielding a pencil and paper, but it made her feel better.

"Do you have more of the proof to explain?" Mark asked.

Abby shrugged. "No."

"Tell me about college."

"It's fine."

"How are your classes?"

"Fine."

"Are they hard?"

Obviously, given the school she went to and her major. "I mean, yeah."

Mark laughed. "Must not be that hard for you. I'm sure your professors are impressed by a brilliant young woman like you."

Abby blushed and looked away. They'd had this conversation many times before, with Abby giving the same one-word answers. But Mark had always quickly pivoted to talk about his prowess in college, about how he was top of his class.

"I'm impressed by you, Abby."

Abby blinked furiously. "Thank you."

"I'm sorry I won't be able to see you graduate."

"It's ok."

"Do you know what you want to do?"

"If I can... I guess I want to do research in pure mathematics."

Mark smiled. He put a hand on hers, squeezing. “You’ll be an amazing mathematician.”

"Because of you," Abby whispered. For once, it didn't hurt her to admit it. She walked to the kitchen and ran the tap, noisily washing her hands so he wouldn't hear her crying.

#

Saturday night, after Abby had presented a steaming clay pot of stewed pork ribs and soy sauce eggs, Mark looked her in the eyes.

“The doctor says I don’t have much longer to live.”

“I’m sorry,” Abby whispered into a bite of egg.

“You have nothing to be sorry about, Abby.”

“I do.”

Vivienne set her chopsticks on the rim of her bowl, clapping her hands. “Let’s talk about something else. Abby’s been doing so well in college.”

Abby locked eyes with Mark. “I’m sorry I said I never loved you.”

“Why would you say that?”

“You don’t remember?”

“Abby.” Vivienne placed her hand next to Abby’s bowl, fingers splayed. Quiet. “Mark, do you want rice cake?”

Mark brushed away the question. “Why did you say that?”

Abby held his gaze, ignoring the tears blurring her vision, hoping Vivienne didn’t scold her for her show of emotion. “Because… because you never cared about me. You never bothered to know anything about me. I was never good enough for you.”

Mark stared at her, wordless.

Abby stood, wiping the tears from her cheeks with the heel of her hand. “I’m sorry. I just. I have to go.”

She fled up the stairs.

She’d barely had time to catch her breath before Vivienne rapped on her door. “Abby, open the door.” Vivienne tried the door knob.

Abby unlocked the door and retreated to her bed.

“I told you not to talk about it.”

“I just —”

“Now he’s going to be agitated. Do you want that to be what he’s thinking about as he’s dying?”

“I—”

“No?”

“No.”

“Don’t talk about it anymore, understand?”

“I understand.”

“Good.” Vivienne left the door ajar behind her.

After she heard Vivienne’s footsteps retreat, Abby walked to the door to close it again, but stopped when she saw Mark's silhouette in the hallway. 

“Thank you for explaining the theorem to me,” Mark said.

Abby froze. “You’re welcome.”

“What were you and your mother talking about?”

“Nothing.”

“I don’t remember what happened.”

“It’s fine.”

“I’m sorry.”

Abby turned away. “Not much you can do about it now.”

“I’m sorry. I was trying my best. I was always trying my best.”

Abby looked at him — properly took him in, for the first time since she’d arrived. His hands were shoved deep into the pockets of his slacks. His shirt was crumpled, the top button undone. 

“It’s ok.” Abby pressed the door shut quietly.

“Goodnight Abby,” Mark said through the door.

“Goodnight.”

#

Abby was sipping a cup of tea and reviewing her proof for submission when her mother called. She’d been drinking the brew more — so much so that she'd run out soon. She'd ask her friend where they’d gotten it from. Something about it felt warm. Like home.

“It happened,” Vivienne said, her voice cracking.

Abby stared into the steaming mug. It smelled like her childhood, before Mark left. “I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be.”

Abby set the phone down. Her hands shook. The cursor on the monitor blinked. On. Off. Abby wiped her eyes with the back of her hand and typed out the last line of her acknowledgements: "The author would like to thank her late father, Mark Li, for his foundational work in the field and assistance with the finalization of this proof."
