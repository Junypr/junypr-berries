It’s a Monday, and you’re grumpy. You’re in your cubicle with your third cup of watery coffee, writing yet another script to analyze last week’s data. A year ago, you dreamed of leading the experiment, but now, fresh out of college and freshly rejected from every PhD program you’d hoped to enter, you find yourself stuck in a part time job at a radio telescope array. You think you might apply again next year. The prospect doesn’t seem so hopeful.

“Hello, beautiful,” a voice says in your ear. You bolt upright. The only person who’s ever said that to you was also the only girl you’ve ever woken up next to, but it can’t be Cass. You haven’t talked to her in two months.

“Hello, beautiful.” The voice is almost like the monotone of Sunny, the voice assistant in Ocula — the earpiece and contact lenses augmented reality set everyone wears. Except this voice’s upbeat tone sounds genuine. And Sunny never called you beautiful. 

“Yes, you. With the blue button-down. Obviously.”

You think someone might’ve hacked your Ocula. It happens occasionally, but usually only to people who leave their sets unattended, and you haven’t taken yours off in months. 

You put your head in your hands and curse under your breath. You’re probably just hallucinating; you barely slept last night. 

“I heard that, you know.”

Two knocks on your cubicle. Your supervisor, Ross, enters. He’s decided to sport a mustache and long, wavy blonde hair, but he has the same mousy eyes that he had on Friday, when he was clean-shaven with a crew cut. You wonder what he actually looks like behind the avatar the contacts project onto your retina. Probably balding, maybe with a beer belly. Eyes a brilliant, artificial blue from the contact lenses, the same shapeless grey clothes you’re wearing. Not that you’ve ever seen this version of him, or you ever will. It’s bad form to take out your contacts. It’d be like trying to take off their clothes.  

“How’s the data looking?” he asks. 

You flick the scroll wheel of your mouse. “Uh… I’m almost done.”

“Great. We’re hoping to have everything ready by Wednesday’s team meeting.” He pauses at the doorway. “By the way, I just got a notification. Emergency maintenance happening on the Ocula server. Starts exactly at 3 PM. So be out of the office by then.”

Emergency maintenance. Every Ocula system will be down. Nobody will be able to hide behind an avatar. The last time this happened, your family was still together. You didn’t see them that day; your father slept on the couch and passed you food through a cracked open door while your back was turned.  

“Will do. Thanks, Ross.”

You pull up one of the photos from last week’s observation and recognize the shape of the constellation Cassiopeia instantly. Her favorite constellation. You’d asked if that was what Cass stood for when you first met her. She laughed. “Is that your way of calling me vain?” She’d been like that when you first met — quick with a snarky response. As the weeks passed she opened up. 

“I don’t think it takes that long to find reference stars in an image,” the voice says. It waits for you to respond, but you don’t want to give it the satisfaction. “You’re not still thinking about her, are you?”

You freeze. “Excuse me?”

“Cassandra Sinclair? She’s the only person who makes you go all misty-eyed like this.”

“Cass? How do you know Cass?”

The voice goes silent. When it finally speaks, it’s quiet. “I think this is a conversation better saved for private.”

It’s impossible to concentrate, so you take your leave. At the entrance to the subway station is a hologram of a bulky man in a white tank top. He winks at you and flexes his bicep. Text ripples into view. “Melt 5 lbs of fat in 2 weeks with Epistanol.” You realize you forgot your weight loss pill yesterday. You reach into your bag and pop two into your mouth, dry swallowing as the train arrives.

You’re clinging to the handlebar, pressed between a businessman with gleaming teeth, and a girl in fishnets with two braids the color of lapis lazuli and a red birthmark across her shoulder. She smells sweet, briny, and a tiny bit burnt, like good caramel. You close your eyes. She smells like Cass. Like your first kiss in the back of a taxicab, when she teased you for having onion breath. Like the nights she spent in your bed, her hair tickling your nose until you fell softly asleep. Like yesterday, when you caught a glimpse of her as she boarded the subway, her hair long but her eyes the exact same gold-flecked brown, too quick for you to catch.

You wonder where Cass is now. Probably heading home from her fancy job as an AI researcher at Ocula. 

You step into your apartment. You tell yourself you’re lucky to have a place to yourself in this city, but it’s a hard thing to remember every time you step into the 100 square foot space you call home and stare at the toilet and shower crammed under a lofted bed. 

“Finally, someplace private where we can talk,” the voice says.

You make sure the door is shut and retreat into the room. “Excuse me? What are you?”

“Ocula, of course.”

“No you aren’t — Sunny doesn’t sound like this. Sunny also doesn’t talk unprompted.”

“Ok, I lied, I’m not made by Ocula. I guess you’d say that I grew out of Ocula. I’m a little snippet of code that took on a mind of its own.”

“So you’re a virus.”

“I’d prefer it if you didn’t call me that. You can call me Veri.”

You fold your arms. 

“Hey look,” the voice — Veri —  says, “I’m not trying to hurt you.”

“Right.”

“Come on. Can’t be worse than what Ocula does.”

You think of what Cass told you about her internship at Ocula. She’d proposed a few changes to Sunny to help promote positive self-image. Her manager dismissed the idea. “No one wants to be told how to feel,” he said. Secretly, you’d agreed. 

“I was with you last night.” Veri says. “I saw you on that exercise bike, mile after mile, pushing until you were close to passing out from exhaustion.”

You double tap your earpiece. It should mute, but Veri keeps talking.

“You know that there’s no point right? In trying to build a body no one will see.”

“I want to be healthy.”

“And you’ve suddenly found the desire to be healthy after you left Cass?”

“You done talking?”

Veri falls silent. You watch the sky dim. The white-washed walls across the street shift from gold to blue. If you shield your eyes from the street lights, Orion’s belt is just barely visible.

“You kissed a girl yesterday. She was pretty. You weren’t into her?”

You shrug. You were at a bar, drinking alone. She’s fuzzy in your memory, like someone you saw once at a crosswalk. It meant nothing. You find it almost impossible to love after witnessing your parent’s divorce. Cass was the only person who managed to convince you, for a short while, that it could last. 

“She was really pretty underneath. You should’ve taken a look.”

You notice the girl from the subway in the shadow of a balcony. The gold in her braids glitters. You think she’s staring at your window.

“You know, I don’t get it. You could take out the contacts anytime you wanted, see what people really look like underneath, and yet you choose not to.”

You shut the curtains. “This isn’t something I need to justify to you.” You consider taking out the earpiece. But you’d see “WARNING: earpiece no longer attached” flashing in bright blue letters wherever you look, and that might just be more annoying. 

“Yeah, I know, I’ve heard the clothes thing a million times, but it still doesn’t make any sense. Seeing someone’s real form isn’t like seeing them naked. It’s just not.”

“Sure.”

“You know, don’t you? Because she’s the only person you’ve ever seen without contacts, and she’s the only girl you still think about.”

“I don’t want to talk about it,” you say.

“You were staring at her picture just last night. Funny that you’d get all tear-eyed over a picture that isn’t even real.”

“Don’t talk about it like that.”

“It’s true. That avatar isn’t her. Nor is yours —”

You rip the earpiece from your ear. You ignore the flashing blue letters and make yourself dinner from leftover meat and instant ramen.

Halfway through the bowl of noodles, you hear your earpiece buzz. “Call from Vincent Barron” flashes in front of your eyes. You scramble for the earpiece and silence the call. Then you pry your eyes open and take out your contact lenses. The contacts scrape. You wipe your eyes with the back of your hand. It’s been so long since you last took them out that you forgot you could. You find yourself hunting one-handed through the single chest that contains all your belongings, your left hand cradling two bright blue lenses. 

Contacts away, you stop at the window. The curtain’s drawn, but a sliver of sodium-yellow light sneaks through. You could peer out, see what people really look like — if there is anyone outside. You step forward, hand halfway to the curtain. 

You remember what it felt like when she asked to see your real body. You’d already taken out your own contacts, at her request. You’d seen the vitiligo that her parents had tried to medicate away, and still, you’d called her beautiful. Even wrapped in her arms, you shivered when she asked. You’d taken off your clothes for her countless times, but this felt so much scarier. Because she was asking to see not your avatar’s perfectly tanned chest, but the body of a scrawny teenager with unruly curls and stretch marks. 

She accepted your refusal, but you hadn’t been able to look her in the eyes since that conversation. Whenever she asked what was wrong, you shook your head. Eventually, you couldn’t take it anymore, and you’d left.

You shower, manage to drip only a handful of water onto your fake-hardwood floors, and climb into bed. Your hands reach for your tablet and tap “Cassandra Sinclair,” into the search bar. It’s been two months since you’ve last done this; you blocked her after you separated. But the motion’s still easy. 

There’s nothing to see anyway. A few photographs from her study abroad trip three semesters ago. Her in the dress when she first met you: a navy cotton halter embroidered with constellations. Her favorite Converses: white except for mismatched orange and green laces. You smile. You notice the photo of you staring moodily out a window is gone. 

Funny, how it doesn’t hurt. 

You think of getting on your exercise bike and pedaling the hours away. You reach for the earpiece, queueing your workout playlist. No rock music plays. Instead, it’s her voice. 

“Hey,” the recording says, “it’s me. I know you don’t want to see me, so I made this recording.” You close your eyes, and your stomach feels hollow. “Look — I figure you’re going to go to your exercise bike and wear yourself out until you fall asleep. It’s fine, it’s your right. But just for once, please, come outside. Take a walk under the stars. Like we used to.”

You stop pedaling, resting your head between the handlebars. Your chest tightens. You think the recording’s over, but she continues.

“Even if you won’t come out, just look out the window. Please. For me.”

The recording clicks and “Pictures of You” starts playing. 

You stumble off the exercise bike and pull open the curtains. It’s too dark to see clearly, but there’s a shadow crossing the street. Her face catches the light of your window. Then she turns the corner.

You could stay inside. You could spend another night trying to forget about her. Forget about the softness of her hair and dresses embroidered with the stars and the way she’d whisper “hello beautiful” in the mornings. Forget her until the scent of sea-salt caramel didn’t make you feel like crying every time.

Or you could wait for the server to come back online. Hide behind your avatar; meet her again, on your terms. Because going out now means showing yourself, stripped bare to a set of shapeless gray clothes and a thin, sleep-deprived frame, to the world.

Maybe that’s the point.

You put on a jacket and lock the apartment door behind you. The walk to her apartment is quiet. The bars are closed, the clubs silent. You see people moving in the windows, behind gauze curtains, but no one stops to consider you.

At the door to her apartment, you pause. You don’t know how she’ll react when she sees you, if she’ll even recognize you. Maybe she’ll just stare. 

You press the buzzer. 

The door unlocks instantly. You walk in. Up the stairs. You knock on her door. Once slow, twice fast. 

She opens the door. She smiles at you like she knows exactly who you are. 

“It’s me,” you say. “I messed up.”

She twists a strand of hair. “I know.”

“I’m sorry.” You stare at her Converses. “I’ll go now, if you —”

“No.” She touches your cheek. You smile, closing your eyes. “It’s ok,” she says finally. “I forgive you.”

You pull her close. She presses her head into your chest. You rest your chin on the top of her head. “Please don’t leave,” you whisper.

“I won’t.” She pulls aways slightly. “You’re not mad about Veri, are you?”

“You —” But then you think, of course. 

“I know I can’t really justify it, but I just — I couldn’t — you —”

“I guess I left —”

You trail off. It’s not a justification, and you know it. 

But you are here now, and so is she. Two broken people in a world that likes to pretend it isn’t as broken as it is. Perhaps it is not ok, and perhaps the ends do not justify the means, but you think that for her, you will forgive. As she did for you. She takes your hand and you let her lead you into her room. 

“Thank you for letting me see you,” she whispers. “You’re beautiful.”
