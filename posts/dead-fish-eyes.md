A man sits in a room that is white and square. Soft light emanates from all four walls, and it fades once a day so that he may sleep, but there is no window, so he does not really know what time it is. The nurse that brings him meals thrice a day says it's been four months. He feels like it's been longer.

She has set his first meal of the day — breakfast, if one can call it that — on a low table. There is a fillet of overcooked tilapia, a bowl of spinach and kale, a glass of water, and a handful of pink pills. There is no coffee, because that might harm the child. No salt or salad dressing, because that, too, would be unhealthy. But at least they’ve tried to give him some variety.

He sits on the ground, cross-legged, and eats with the single fork they’ve given. There is no knife. There has never been a knife. Nor other blades, nor ropes, nor hooks, nor bars, in the room. He has checked.

The nurse does not make eye contact with him, nor does she address him by name. She turns to inspect the tank in the center of the room. The tank is connected to him by a flexible tube at his navel, which hasn't left his skin in months. From his belly, through the tube, and into the tank flow blood vessels. At their end is the child. It is red, and has spindly limbs, an oversized head, and hands too big for its wrists.

“It's growing fast,” she says. “That's good. You've been eating enough?” 

He nods. A few weeks ago, when the nausea peaked, he wrapped a half-eaten fish fillet in a bundle of tissues and tucked it in the trash. The stench the next day tipped the nurse off, and she scolded him.

Even if he couldn't keep it down, he'd done his best to eat everything since.

The nurse circles the tank. “How have you been feeling?” 

“Alright, I guess. I'm a little sore. Mostly my feet and back hurt.” 

She nods. “That's normal.” 

“I suppose.” 

He doesn't tell her about the other stuff. About how some afternoons, the weight of everything falls upon him, and he can feel it in his chest, and sometimes, when no one's watching, he cries in big hiccuping sobs like he hasn't since he was four. He thinks she knows. She probably knows. But she won't do anything unless it harms the child.

She smiles at him. “You'll be a good father.” 

#

He's always wanted to be a father. He isn't sure why. He hasn't spent much time around them, being an only child, but it just feels natural. And his parents want grandchildren anyway.

Not many women agree with him. Most his age aren't interested in men, or choose to be sterilized. The process is simple, safe, and cheap — unlike pregnancy, wanted or not.

So he counts himself lucky to be in a long-term relationship with Sunny. She's pretty, gracious to his parents, and as far as he knows, fertile. His parents love her. They welcomed her into their home, and told her that they'd have such beautiful babies together. Sunny was quiet on the matter.

Until his mother was diagnosed with cervical cancer, and the little money they had wasn't enough to treat her and support a family.

There was a wealthy couple in the town, trying for a child. They needed someone else to carry it. They offered two million dollars for a female surrogate; five hundred thousand for a man. He begged Sunny to take the offer, but she refused, saying that she didn't want to leave her job. A month later, he was laid off from his accounting role. He took the child.

#

Sunny's visiting this afternoon.

The parents allow an hour every day, and she used to come almost that often. But as the months passed, her visits grew more infrequent. Today would be the first time in a week.

When she arrives, she smells of wood dust and singed plastic, and he knows from her grimy steel-toed boots that she came directly from work. She’s an electrician. She’s been wiring a series of new homes.

After he introduced her to his family, his parents none-too-subtly suggested she quit her job and stay at home. She was too pretty to work so hard, they said. Her skin would be burnt from the hours in the sun. But she insisted.

“Hi darling.” 

“Hey.” Sunny kneels to unlace her boots.

He waits for her to say something. Finally, after lining her boots up in the corner: “How has your day been?” 

“Alright. The nurse said it’s all going well.” 

“Oh really? That’s good.” Sunny inspects the tank, making a face at the thing inside. “God, it’s so — it’s hideous. It’s got dead fish eyes.” 

“I wouldn't say that. I think it'll be beautiful when it's ready.” 

“Really? I think it’ll be evil and twisted and ugly like its parents.” 

He glances at the ceiling. It's perfectly smooth and blank, but from the way the nurse acts, he suspects someone's always watching. “They might be listening.” 

Sunny sighs. “Fine,” she says loudly, “it's the most beautiful thing to have ever walked this planet.” 

She stares at him. “I don't know why you do this to yourself. I know your parents need the money, but —” 

“Sunny, I couldn't find a job. Still can't find a job. And this — I guess it's not so bad.” He smiles at her. “I get food, a nice bed, infinite time. You could've done it, Sunny. It's probably better suited for you than your current job. And they'd pay you more than me.” 

“I can't.” 

“But you're not sterilized.” 

“I told you already. I didn't get it done because I have a medical condition. The risk of complications is too high.” 

“How does that stop you from —” 

“The risk of complications for that is too high, too.” 

He stares at the baby in the tank. It's spinning, slowly, twisting the blood vessels that connect him to it. He feels a tugging at his navel and grimaces.

“My parents want grandchildren,” he says, slowly.

“Do you want children?” 

“Of course I want children. Doesn't everyone?” 

“Do you really?” 

“Yes. Don't you?” 

“No.” 

He stares at Sunny. “But I thought you —” 

“I don't even like them. So I'm certainly not going to risk my life for them.” 

“I—” 

“I'm sorry if you expected something else. But I just can't. Sorry.” 

“Hey,” he says, reaching for her hand. His hands are cold and swollen, and although she tries to hide it, she flinches when he touches her.

“What?” 

“Can I get a kiss?” 

“Did you even listen to what I said? Because I swear this isn't the first time I've told you.” 

“I heard you. I just — Can you —” 

She closes her eyes, then nods and leans in. Just before their lips meet, she turns away, and his lips land on her cheek.

“I’m sorry.” She pulls back. “My breath smells bad.” 

“I don’t mind.” 

“I just — is it ok if I don’t really want to right now?” 

He looks at the ground. “That’s fine.” 

“I’m sorry, I —” 

“You're upset with me, aren't you?” 

“I mean, not entirely. I’ve had a long —” 

“I haven’t been feeling great, Sunny. Everything hurts and my brain is foggy and my feet are too big for my shoes and I can’t stop crying—” 

Sunny takes a tissue from the box on the table and catches the tears on his cheeks. “I’m sorry. I’m so sorry.” She places a kiss on his forehead. “It’s all normal. It’ll be over soon.” 

“Thank you.” He wipes away the last of the tears with the back of his hand, hiccuping. “Do you love me?” 

“Of course I love you.” Sunny smiles. Then she looks away.

Sunny sits in a chair and reads for the remainder of the hour. Normally, she reads the news or a pop science book on space, but she’s been feeling pretty cheesy recently, so it’s a cute fantasy romance. She can't concentrate on it, though. She's angry. At herself, for not leaving earlier. At him, for being just barely enough that she's stayed this long.

#

He takes a bath in the tub behind a curtain. It's fake privacy, he thinks, if there are cameras, but he finds that he can't even care anymore, because they've already claimed his body for their own use. So what if they see him undressed.

He's careful to wash gently around where the tube connects to his belly, just like the nurse instructed. The tube started small — barely thicker than a pencil — but has grown to cover a palm-sized space centered around his navel. The skin inside is red and moist, and he tries not to think about what will happen when the tube is finally removed. At least it doesn't hurt.

He hasn't looked in a mirror for months, but after he dries off, he finds that his once-loose pajamas are just a little too tight on him. His skin has become splotchy, and thin red veins crawl across the backs of his hands. He fingers a pimple on his chin. He'd never been attractive to begin with — slumped shoulders, acne, and flab — but in the past weeks, everything seemed to have gotten a little worse. It makes him sick.

He slips into bed, naked, and tries to sleep. A dim red light illuminates the tank. He watches the child float within the tank, spinning and spinning as sleep slowly claims him. The last time he opens his eyes, half awake, he sees that the tank is empty.

He sits up, heart pounding. The child has floated into the tube and is slowly drifting towards him.

He throws off the blanket, grabs the tube in front of the child, and kinks it around a chair. The child stops swimming and slowly retreats.

He returns to bed, using the breathing exercises the nurse taught him to calm his heart.

He wonders, not for the first time, if Sunny's found another man. Someone from her work that smells of leather welding jackets and fresh-cut birch planks. He knows the kind of ruggedness that she’s drawn to. The kind that he can’t find in a bottle of cologne, no matter how expensive.

In his heart, he knows why she’s still with him. His parents offered a house bigger than any she’d ever lived in — with rose bushes and a gardener and a lawn enclosed by a white picket fence — and all they asked for was a child. They were well-off — but not wealthy enough to shell out tens of millions for the hordes of grandchildren they were hoping to have. Sunny was their ticket. Except Sunny has other plans.

#

The parents are in the room before he's woken up. The man is barrel-chested and wearing a too-tight suit. The woman is svelte and seems a few years too young for him — but maybe it’s just the procedures she’s had to freeze her face in time. Normally, they ignore him, but today, the woman is staring at him.

“The tube must always lie flat and smooth,” she says. “No kinks. The nurse told you this.” 

“I'm sorry, Ma'am.” 

“How long was it left like this? All night?” 

There's no point in lying. They've probably seen on the cameras. “Yes, Ma'am. I'm very sorry.” 

“You could harm the child — or worse.” 

“We could sue you for damages,” the father says. “You signed a contract.” 

“I'm terribly sorry, both of you. This won't happen again.” 

“It's an honor to be helping to bring a new life into this world. Remember that.” 

He stares at her nude pumps. “Yes, Ma'am".

#

His parents call later that morning. Their faces peer at him through the screen. His dad has his arm around his mom's shoulders.

“Mom. How are you doing?” 

“Could be better. But at least it doesn't hurt so much anymore.” She smiles.

“The doctor says she has a few more years at least,” his father says.

“That's good.” He smiles.

“How're you doing? How's the child?” 

“Fine. Not feeling great, but it's normal, I guess.” 

His mother frowns. “That sounds awful. I don't understand why Sunny doesn't do this. It's supposed to be her job. And she'd get so much more money for it.” 

He shrugs. “I got laid off. And I guess she didn't want to.” 

His mother presses her lips together. “Still seems a little unfair. Well. We were thinking that it's about time you started looking for an engagement ring. You've been with her three years, haven't you?” 

“I guess so.” 

“What's the hesitation? Don't you like her?” 

“I do.” 

“Well, your father and I aren't getting any younger. Nor are you and her. Especially her, if you want kids. And we really want you to.” 

“I'm 33. She's 29. We're not that old.” 

“Well.” His mother laughs. “You know it gets harder every year you wait.” 

“And she's such a beauty now,” his father says. “Those long black curls, those baby cheeks. Truly exotic. She won't be so beautiful if you keep waiting.” 

His mother nudges him. “You can't say that.” She smiles. “But you will have beautiful children. I just hope they have your blue eyes.” 

He smiles. “Mom, really, I don't know.” 

“What do you mean?” 

“About having children.” 

“About having children? But you want them. You've always wanted them.” 

“I do. But Sunny doesn't.” 

“What? What do you mean? But she isn't sterilized.” 

“I don't really understand. She just said she doesn't want to.” 

His father leans in. “Is there any chance she'll change her mind? Lots of women change their minds when they get older, you know.” 

He shrugs. “I don't know. Maybe.” 

His mother and father exchange glances. “Honey,” she says quietly, “I know Sunny's a wonderful person, and you love her a lot, but it may be time to start looking for another person.” 

“I —” he stares. “Mom, I —” 

“You don't have to tell her just yet. No need to make things awkward.” 

“But isn't that —” 

“You're a handsome, capable young man. I'm sure you'll be able to find someone else who's interested.” 

He opens his mouth. Thinks about what he could say — that Sunny's the first woman to have gone on more than a single date with him in years. That he isn't, in fact, handsome, or capable, or desirable.

“Think about it, honey.” And they hang up.

#

Lunch is a tuna salad. It's bad, really bad, because they probably didn't even use real eggs for the mayo. But that's not what makes him sad.

When he first met Sunny, he tried to impress her with a medium-rare steak. He thought she just didn't like food because of how disinterested she was as she nibbled at it. But then she told him that all she wanted was a simple tuna salad sandwich, and since, he’s perfected his recipe. Canned tuna imported from Spain, squeezed to make sure it isn’t watery. Homemade mayo. Black olives. Salt and pepper. She loves it.

He can't finish the tuna salad. It's soggy, and weirdly sweet. He tries to shove another forkful into his mouth, and gags, eyes watering.

He doesn't want to leave Sunny. It hurts him to even think about a life without her. So he thinks about disobeying his parents, not telling Sunny any of this. It'd be easy. And easy is all he's done, all his life.

But somehow, this thought does not cheer him up. He could stay with Sunny, tell her nothing, but eventually his parents will notice. There's nothing he can do, no perfect solution he can think of now, and so he thinks he really should be resting, but he just can't, his mind spooling and spooling back on itself.

He wants to hit himself, to squeeze his eyes shut, stop the tears from flowing, but now he's sobbing, not bothering to catch the tears before they fall.

The next bite of tuna salad is a little briny. He gags again, spitting it into a napkin. Then he rinses his mouth out with the glass of water and leaves the bowl of tuna salad at the center of the room. The nurse comes in a few minutes later to retrieve the tray. She looks at him, but doesn't say anything. He knows she'll tell the parents the next time she sees them. But right now, he doesn't care.

He picks up his phone when the nurse leaves. “Sunny. Sunny? Please Sunny, can we talk?” 

“Hello?” Sunny's voice is hoarse, like she's been shouting, which is unusual for her. “You know I'm at work, right?” 

“I just — I'm so sorry Sunny, I just feel terrible. I needed to hear your voice” 

“What?” 

“I — I feel —” 

“Wait. I'm so sorry. I need to go now. I'll talk to you when I come by this afternoon.” 

She hangs up, and he waits.

#

Sunny comes a little earlier than usual. She unlaces her shoes and stands a few paces away from him, arms folded. “What's going on?” 

“Sunny.” He smiles. “I missed you.” 

“It's only been a day.” 

“Are you mad at me?” 

“No.” Sunny looks away.

“Sunny...” 

She stares at the wall behind him. “I feel like you aren't telling me something. You were upset when you called. What's going on?” 

“It's nothing.” 

“No. No it's not. Don't act like that. Don't lie to me.” 

“I—” 

“Stop fucking lying to me.” 

He snaps his head up, meeting her eyes. Her expression softens when she sees his face, and he feels himself start to cry again. He considers telling her about his parents, about how he's been feeling lately, about everything. Getting it all off his chest.

“I'm sorry, Sunny.” 

“It's ok. You can tell me.” 

“My parents...” 

“Did you tell them I don't want kids?” 

He nods, wiping his nose.

“Well. That's alright.” Sunny sighs, dusting off her already clean hands.

“I'm so sorry Sunny. I know I shouldn't be crying about this. I've just been feeling so terrible recently.” 

“Look. It's fine.” 

He smiles at her. Then his eyes land on the silver sun charm hanging just below her collarbone, and he frowns. “Why are you wearing that?” 

“This?” She fingers the charm. “I like wearing it.” 

“Didn't Marcus make that for you?” 

She told him about the jeweler once, before realizing how uncomfortable the topic made him. Marcus, and many of the other men she's been with, gave her trinkets in their time together, which she's kept out of sight in a dusty cardboard box at his insistence. No matter how much Sunny reassures him, he always feels a little insecure — like the chocolates he gives her can never compare.

“Yes, and it’s beautiful.” 

He doesn't say anything, so Sunny continues.

“I want to talk to you about something.” 

“Ok. I'm ready.” 

“I don't think we're compatible.” 

“What?” 

Sunny takes a breath. “You and your parents have this perfect little dream of a white picket fence and four kids, and I'm just not the person for that. I thought I was, but I've realized now. And even if I tried, you're never going to fully accept me.” 

“Sunny, I— I've always accepted you. You know that, right?” 

“I don't really think so.” 

“What?” 

“I just — you know your parents always made weird comments about me, right? About how I looked, about how they didn't want me to work, about my body, about kids... And you never once defended me.” 

“I'm sorry I— You never said.” 

“But I did.” 

“You can't expect me to pick up on every little thing.” 

“You know, maybe I can. Because my friends do that for me. And Marcus. And the others. Most of them. And they don't try to use me as their therapist. They don't ask for everything from me and give nothing back.” 

“Sunny, I don't do that. I don't ask that much of you.” 

“You asked me to risk my health for children your parents want.” 

“Sunny, I didn’t — This is about how I look, isn't it? About all of this. About how you don't see me anymore. Sunny, please, I promise it'll be over soon, and everything will go back to normal.” 

Sunny shakes her head. “No. No, it's not about this. It's about how you don't respect me. How you don't listen to me, or —” 

“If I hurt you Sunny, I'm really sorry. Please just tell me next time, ok? I'll try to be better.” 

Sunny shakes her head. “It doesn't matter. I don't want your apology.” 

“Sunny, what are you —” 

“I'm sorry this has to be so hard for you. But I can't live with this anymore. I need you out of my life.” 

She ties her shoelaces without looking back at him. “Goodbye,” she says to the door, and shuts it softly behind her.

#

He's trying to sleep, but all he can think about is a knife edge. Something sharp. Something that can cut him free.

He pulls out his phone and calls Sunny. He knows she's home, but he gets sent straight to voicemail.

“Sunny? I know you're mad at me. But please. Why? Why didn't you tell me anything? Why didn't you let me fix it?” 

He hangs up, stares at the wall, and then presses the call button again.

“I just feel so alone. And sad. I can't stop crying, goddamnit. Please Sunny. Please. Don't go.” 

“Sunny. I don't know what to do. I don't have anyone to talk to other than you. Please, tell me what I should do.” 

“I don't know anything anymore.” 

He stares at the child floating in the tank, at the five hundred thousand dollars he will get if he stays here another five months. He wouldn't have to do anything. Just sit here, eat, and sleep. So easy.

Except it's not easy, sitting here, staring at the thing he wants but can never have. Sinking deeper and deeper and deeper. What was the point, anyway, with Sunny gone? His parents need help, sure, but he can find work. He doesn't need that sum for a child anymore.

He walks over to the tank and punches it. The walls of the tank flex. The child keeps spinning.

#

When the nurse brings breakfast, he asks for a razor. She gives him an electric shaver, and he shakes his head. He wants something sharper, smoother. With a blade. But he doesn't say that. He says he wants to trim his hair, and he wants to do it himself.

“I'll have to ask the parents,” she says. She returns an hour later with a razor with sharp blades.

He shaves his face, making eye contact with the nurse through each stroke. Then he trims the back of his head. It's choppy, but when he sees himself in the mirror, he feels fresh.

He looks at the nurse and gestures at the space between his legs. “May I...” 

She nods and turns her back. He steps into the bathtub and draws the curtain.

He doesn't shave himself. Instead, he untwists the head of the razor. The blade falls into the bathtub. He pinches it between finger and thumb. The blade is clean and sharp. He breathes.

In one motion, he slices the tube at his navel and throws the blade onto the ground. Fluid gushes from the tank through the tube, into the tub. He slumps, whimpering, one hand clasping his stomach. He thinks it shouldn't hurt as much as it does.

Sometime later, the nurse screams.

#

He's been lying in the tub long enough that his hands have gone cold when the parents arrive. The father is in a golf tee, his slacks stained with grass. The mother clucks her tongue.

“Call his girlfriend,” she tells the secretary next to her. “And his parents. Then get an ambulance. I don't want him dying on us. Can't let it look like negligence.” 

The secretary nods, and turns, staring at the floor.

She looks at the nurse. “Will it be ok?” 

The nurse shakes her head. “Not this early. I'm sorry.” 

The father glares at him. “We could charge you with manslaughter.” 

He grimaces. Every breath hurts. “I know.” 

“We're not paying for the medical bills. You violated the contract. You take the responsibility.” 

“I know.” 

“I just don't understand,” the mother says. “It's so easy. We give you food, medicine, a place to sleep. You don't have to do anything. And yet you still chose this.” 

“So? Are you going to apologize?” The father's breathing hard, his hands balled into fists.

He meets the father’s eyes. “No. I'm not sorry.” 

He stares. He pulls back his fist. Then he drops it. “Fine. Fine.” 

He waits for an ambulance to pick him up and take him to the hospital.**dead-
